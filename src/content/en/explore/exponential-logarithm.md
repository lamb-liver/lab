## Idea

For $a>0$ and $a\neq 1$, the exponential $y=a^x$ and the logarithm $y=\log_a x$ are inverses of each other. The base $e\approx 2.718$ is the one continuous compounding and calculus use.

This page keeps that relationship and only changes the reading. First read it as a mirror across $y=x$. Then put the vertical axis on a log scale, so doubling becomes a constant slope. Then use area to see why $e$ is natural. One model, in more detail, is left to the single works linked below.

## Interaction

- **Exponential and logarithm**: move **Base a** and **Coefficient b**. On **Linear coordinates**, $y=b\cdot a^x$ and $y=\log_a(x/b)$ mirror each other across $y=x$. Switch the axis button to **log y coordinates** and that same exponential becomes a straight line of slope $\ln a$
- **Birth of e**: raise **Partitions n** and watch $(1+1/n)^n$ approach $e$. Move **Area endpoint x** until the area under $y=1/x$ is $1$
- **Change of base**: change **Base a**. Every $\log_a x$ passes through $(1,0)$, and the base only rescales the graph through $\log_a x=\ln x/\ln a$

Suggested order: Exponential and logarithm, then Birth of e, then Change of base.

## What to notice

- The exponential and the logarithm are symmetric across $y=x$. On log y coordinates, doubling on that same curve becomes a constant slope.
- Changing the axis does not change the values. It changes what an equal step means: a fixed difference on a linear axis, and a fixed ratio on a log axis.
- The mirror and the log axis are still the shape of the same exponential. What is special about $e$ is which base makes a unit area match a unit logarithm, not a more curved graph.
